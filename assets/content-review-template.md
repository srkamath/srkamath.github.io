# Historical Event Content Review Template

Use this checklist for one timeline event or a batch. Review the timeline card, full article, people and landmark profiles, maps, images, source list, cross-references, and print output—not just the opening summary.

Record each item as **Pass**, **Changes required**, or **Flagged for research/user decision**.

## 1. Independent fact-checking

- [ ] Verify every proper name, date, place, title, and relationship against at least one cited source; do not treat another timeline article as independent confirmation.
- [ ] Compare important claims across more than one authoritative source where practical.
- [ ] Check that the source actually supports the claim, not merely the general topic.
- [ ] Mechanically compare quotations with the source text where a readable source is available.
- [ ] Remove or qualify claims that rest only on a weak, derivative, or inaccessible source.

## 2. Confidence and evidence separation

- [ ] The confidence status—firm, approximate, traditional, or contested—matches the evidence.
- [ ] Attested fact, scholarly inference, community tradition, and editorial interpretation are visibly distinct.
- [ ] Dates and ranges show uncertainty honestly and do not imply false precision.
- [ ] Undated traditions remain undated rather than being forced into the chronology.
- [ ] Conflicting scholarly readings are named without manufacturing a false consensus.

## 3. Completeness and historical context

- [ ] The article answers what happened, when or why undated, where, who, why it matters, what led to it, and what followed.
- [ ] Every person is introduced with a broader role and significance beyond the local episode.
- [ ] Institutions, communities, dynasties, terms, and practices are explained for a newcomer.
- [ ] A quoted book or inscription is introduced with its author/text, date, genre, context, exact passage, and relevance.
- [ ] Evidence limits and unresolved questions are explicit.
- [ ] Related events are cross-referenced where they clarify continuity or contradiction.

## 4. Cross-article consistency

- [ ] Names, transliteration, titles, dates, dynastic sequences, and place forms match everywhere they recur.
- [ ] No article silently contradicts another.
- [ ] Shared sources use consistent bibliographic details.
- [ ] Timeline card, expanded article, profiles, map notes, references appendix, and print edition agree.
- [ ] Chronological placement follows the project's linear dating system.

## 5. Readability

- [ ] The prose develops a connected historical narrative rather than a list of facts or quotations.
- [ ] The opening establishes why the event matters before introducing detail.
- [ ] Technical and local terms are glossed on first use.
- [ ] First references of relevant topics link to suitable Wikipedia articles for orientation.
- [ ] Paragraph order carries the reader from context to event to consequences.
- [ ] A newcomer can distinguish the principal argument from supporting detail.

## 6. Citations, quotations, and credits

- [ ] Every substantive factual claim is traceable to a numbered citation.
- [ ] Citations resolve to complete bibliography entries with stable URLs where available.
- [ ] Verbatim quotations include exact page, section, inscription, or verse references.
- [ ] Sanskrit passages show Devanagari beside English, with exact parva/adhyaya/verse citations.
- [ ] Image captions state what the image actually depicts and do not imply a false historical connection.
- [ ] Every image, map, quotation, and adapted dataset retains its credit and license information online and in print.

## 7. Source quality

- [ ] Prefer inscriptions, critical editions, archaeological and epigraphical reports, archival records, gazetteers, academic monographs, peer-reviewed scholarship, and official institutional records.
- [ ] Use modern summaries only for orientation, never as the sole support for a disputed claim.
- [ ] Remove blogs, aggregators, unsourced databases, and circular citations unless no stronger source exists; if retained, label the limitation.
- [ ] Distinguish a source contemporary with the event from a later retelling.
- [ ] Record discrepancies between editions, translations, or scholarly readings.

## 8. Layout and rendering

- [ ] Desktop and phone layouts have no horizontal overflow, clipping, overlapping panels, or unreadably small text.
- [ ] The expanded article opens in the intended full-width reading surface and all navigation works.
- [ ] Maps initialize only when needed, stay within their dedicated frames, and do not overlap images or text.
- [ ] Images are relevant, correctly cropped, and web-sized; avoid multi-megabyte originals when an adequate Wikimedia derivative exists.
- [ ] Alt text is meaningful and captions remain legible.
- [ ] The print edition carries the full article, citations, credits, and evidence distinctions; figures and quotations do not split awkwardly across pages.
- [ ] Every book content-page template—including event articles, food essays, registers, and endnotes—uses the full parchment `--paper` background on screen and pure white in print; full-bleed covers are exempt.

## Standing editorial rules

- Keep evidence and interpretation visibly separate.
- Prefer real, reusable images; label later representations honestly.
- Use a strictly linear, consistent dating format.
- Show confidence through icons with accessible hover/focus labels.
- Use story-first layouts for legendary material when standard chronology sections do not fit.
- Never drop credits or references from either the online or print edition.
