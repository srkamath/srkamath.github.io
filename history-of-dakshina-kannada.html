Introduction

Tulunadu: a coast that made its own history

Between the Arabian Sea and the Western Ghats lies a narrow strip of India’s southwest coast. The mountains wall it off from the Deccan plateau; the sea opens it to Arabia, Persia, Europe. Every river that runs down from the Ghats reaches the sea within a few dozen miles, and nearly every river mouth could serve as a harbor. That single fact of geography — closed to the east, open to the west — made everything that follows.

The people here have called this land Tulunadu, the land of the Tulu people, for some two thousand years. The name appears as Tulunatu in the early-Tamil anthology Akananuru (poem 15, by Mamulanar, early centuries CE). Medieval inscriptions across the region variously call it Tuludesa, Tulurajya, Tulumandala, or Tulunadu. It is older than every empire that claimed the coast.

For centuries the coast ruled itself. The native Alupa dynasty held sway roughly from the 7th to the 14th century, and their realm had a political name of its own: Alvakheda, “the land of the Alvas,” styled Alvakheda-6000 in Rashtrakuta- and Hoysala-period inscriptions. The precise meaning of “6000” is uncertain — it belongs to Karnataka’s conventional numbered territorial nomenclature.

Outsiders renamed it, repeatedly. The Portuguese, who sailed this coast in the 1500s, called it Canara. The British, after defeating Tipu Sultan in 1799, made it the district of Canara in the Madras Presidency — the Southern Division colloquially “South Canara,” which became a separate district only with the 1859/60 split, North Kanara going to Bombay in 1862. Dakshina Kannada, the name adopted in January 1977, is simply the Kannada translation of that British label.

This history covers the historic South Canara district — larger than today’s district of that name: Udupi and Dakshina Kannada in Karnataka, Kasaragod in Kerala. Kasaragod was part of South Canara until the linguistic reorganization of 1 November 1956; Udupi district was carved out of Dakshina Kannada only in 1997. The old sources — gazetteers, Alupa and Vijayanagara records, the Basel missionaries’ letters — broadly treat the whole coast as one country, so this timeline does too. The traditional southern boundary holds: the Chandragiri (Payaswini) river, north of which the traveler Francis Buchanan in 1801 was told the country is “called by Hindus as Tuluva, the Tulu country.”

What that geography produced was a culture connected and distinct at once. Tulu, the earliest-branching subgroup of the South Dravidian languages, survived centuries of Kannada, Sanskrit, and colonial administration. Daiva Aradhane — the veneration of guardian spirits through dance and trance — remains a living practice with no exact parallel outside the coast, its closest cousins North Malabar’s theyyam and the Himalayan deity-cult traditions. The matha tradition centered on Udupi’s Krishna Matha — founded in the tradition of the 13th-century philosopher Madhvacharya — organized not just religion but mass feeding, Vedic learning, and the famous temple cuisine. And because every river mouth could be a harbor, the coast was always a trading coast: Arab merchants from the medieval centuries, then Portuguese, Dutch, and English factories, layered new communities — Bearys, Konkani Hindus and Catholics, Jains, Basel mission converts — onto an older Tuluva base. The result is a small region of unusual density: many languages, many faiths, and a history in which a temple, a port, and a river are never far apart.

Sea to crest

Three bands, one connected landscape

01

Coast & estuaries

The shoreline is almost straight but cut by rivers, creeks, sandy ridges, and bays. Estuaries supplied the sheltered water that the open surf denied, so ports and political revenue centers rose where river met sea. This is the geography behind Mangaluru, Malpe, Barkur, Basrur, and Gangolli. Source: IISc Energy & Wetlands Research Group, ETR 50; estuarine complexes after Rao & Suresh (2001).

02

Laterite midlands

Between shore and mountain lies a broken, low plateau of red laterite: acidic, nutrient-poor, and heavily leached by rain. Paddy occupies low valley bottoms; coconut follows the sandy coastal belt and homesteads; areca favors the wetter midland slopes. Cashew later found a place on the poorest uplands. Source: regional agronomy studies and ICAR/PMC arecanut research.

03

Western Ghats

The Sahyadri escarpment rises abruptly to the east. Charmadi, Shiradi, Sampaje, and Bisle are doors through that wall, connecting the coast with Chikkamagaluru, Sakleshpur, Coorg, Mysore, and the Deccan. Their historical use is unevenly documented, but their physical role as conduits is clear. Source: IISc Western Ghats materials; historical use of Bisle remains approximate.

Rivers & ports

Short rivers organize the coast

Most rivers are rain-fed, run west, and change character with the monsoon. The best documented is the Netravati: it rises at roughly 1,000 metres between Kudremukh and Ballalarayana Durga, drains 3,657 square kilometres, and meets the Gurupura at Mangaluru. The 1855 Gazetteer of Southern India called it the Bantwal River and described it as unfordable during the southwest monsoon.

Northward lie the Nandini/Pavanje, Shambhavi, Pangala, Udayavara, Swarna, Sita, and Panchagangavali systems. The Swarna’s often-repeated length of about 55 kilometres comes only from secondary river lists and remains approximate.

  • Netravati–Gurupura: Mangaluru estuary
  • Udayavara: Malpe port
  • Sita: Barkur river system
  • Panchagangavali: Gangolli and Kundapura
  • Chandragiri/Payaswini: traditional southern boundary
  • Swarna: principal Udupi water source; length approximate
The geological past & monsoon

Ninety million years beneath a seasonal sea

The four uninhabited St Mary’s Islands off Malpe preserve columnar rhyolitic lava formed by subaerial volcanism when Madagascar was still attached to India, roughly 90 million years ago. The Geological Survey of India designated them a National Geological Monument in 1975. Vasco da Gama’s supposed landing there in 1498 is repeated as local tradition, not treated here as established fact.

Above this landscape of the geological past, the southwest monsoon sets the human calendar. Published district averages cluster around four metres of rain a year but differ by source and measurement period, so the figure is best read as an approximate range. Paddy follows the rains; rivers swell into torrents; the open-sea fishing season contracts during the roughest June–August weather.

Political geography

The boundary moved; the region persisted

Alupa era

Alvakheda, “land of the Alvas,” is inscriptionally attested, but its precise northern and southern limits remain unsettled.

14th–17th c.

Vijayanagara administration divided the coast into Mangaluru Rajya and Barkuru Rajya, each governed by an Odeya.

1799–1862

The British absorbed Canara after the Fourth Mysore War. Sources disagree on whether its north–south bifurcation belongs to 1859 or 1860; North Canara moved to Bombay Presidency in 1862.

1 Nov 1956

Linguistic reorganization sent Kasaragod to Kerala; the remainder entered Mysore State.

1997

Udupi district was carved out of Dakshina Kannada, creating the present administrative map.

Reading the history through the land

The strongest geographic interpretation is a double movement: the Ghats insulated the coast while a few passes connected it inland; estuaries opened the same coast outward to maritime trade. Heavy rain and leached soil encouraged intensive garden cultivation rather than a vast grain surplus. Those conditions help explain the region’s durable cultural coherence, trading orientation, and long history of migration—but this is an interpretation drawn from the physical evidence, not a single archival claim.

Open questions

The exact Kanara bifurcation year remains 1859 or 1860 depending on the source; Alvakheda’s limits are unsettled; the Swarna’s reported length is secondary; rainfall totals vary by period; and the 1498 St Mary’s landing remains traditional.
